<p>
 Walk into any serious listening room and you'll notice the same thing.
 The owner will talk about the amplifier, brag about the turntable, gush
 over the speakers. The cables? Almost always an afterthought. Yet the
 wires running between those components carry every note the system
 produces, and the wrong ones can quietly hold back everything upstream
 of them.
</p>
<p>
 We've been matching hi-fi cables to Ottawa systems since 1986, from our
 showroom at 903 Carling Avenue — and unlike most of what sits in our
 racks, cables are something we actually build ourselves. Hand-soldered,
 custom-terminated interconnects and speaker cables come off our own
 bench, alongside carried lines from Cardas, Transparent, WBT, and PS
 Audio. Here's what genuinely matters when you're shopping for hi-fi
 cables in Ottawa, what a custom build can do that an off-the-shelf box
 can't, and how to spend wisely rather than just heavily.
</p>
<h2>
 What Makes a Hi-Fi Cable Worth the Investment?
</h2>
<p>
 A cable is, at its core, a conductor wrapped in insulation. Easy
 enough. What separates a $20 lamp-cord from a serious audio cable is
 the care taken over every variable that affects how cleanly an analog
 or digital signal travels between two points.
</p>
<h3>
 Conductor Material and Purity
</h3>
<p>
 Oxygen-free copper (OFC) is the floor, not the ceiling. Higher-tier
 cables step up to long-grain copper, OCC (Ohno Continuous Casting)
 copper, or silver-plated conductors. Cardas, one of our longest-standing
 lines, built its reputation on ultra-pure copper and its distinctive
 golden-ratio stranding, designed to break up the resonances that
 develop inside a conductor bundle. Whether your ears catch the
 difference between conductor grades is a fair question — we'll get to
 that debate below — but the materials themselves are genuinely
 different, and so is the discipline behind them.
</p>
<h3>
 Geometry and Shielding
</h3>
<p>
 Cable geometry is the unsung hero here. The way conductors are twisted,
 spaced, and shielded determines how much electromagnetic and
 radio-frequency interference sneaks into your signal path. Good
 shielding matters more in a downtown condo packed with Wi-Fi routers
 and smart bulbs than it does in a quiet rural house near Almonte, which
 is exactly why cable advice should start with a conversation about
 where and how you listen, not with a price list.
</p>
<h3>
 Termination Quality
</h3>
<p>
 Even a well-built cable with a sloppy solder joint is just an expensive
 failure point. Hand-soldered terminations with silver-content solder
 and properly engineered connectors are the details that separate
 bench-built work from mass-produced spools. It's one reason we carry
 WBT — the German-made locking RCA plugs, spades, and binding posts that
 many flagship cable builders specify — and why every cable that leaves
 our workshop is terminated by hand.
</p>
<h2>
 Custom Cables, Built in Our Ottawa Workshop
</h2>
<p>
 This is the part of the category we're proudest of. Distinctive Audio
 has hand-built and custom-terminated cables in-house for decades — not
 as a sideline, but because it's the only way to get certain jobs done
 properly.
</p>
<h3>
 Tailored Lengths and Terminations
</h3>
<p>
 Off-the-shelf cables are sold in fixed increments. Your actual run is
 rarely one of them. Coiled excess adds inductance you don't want and
 clutter you definitely don't want, while a too-short cable puts
 constant strain on connectors. A custom build solves both: we measure
 your run, build to it, and terminate with the connectors your gear
 actually needs — RCA, XLR, spades, bananas, or something less common.
</p>
<h3>
 Hand-Soldered Connections
</h3>
<p>
 Mass-produced cables are usually machine-crimped. A hand-soldered
 joint, done well, has lower resistance and resists oxidation far
 longer. Rhéal Nadeau, who has run this shop since 1986, came to audio
 from analytical chemistry, and it shows at the bench: conductor
 materials, solder composition, and joint quality are treated as things
 to get measurably right, not as marketing copy.
</p>
<h3>
 Repairs, Re-Terminations, and System Matching
</h3>
<p>
 Because we build cables, we can also fix them. A favourite interconnect
 with a failing plug, a speaker cable that needs new terminations after
 an amplifier change, a balanced run that has to become single-ended —
 these are bench jobs we handle routinely. And before we build anything
 new, we'll ask about your speakers, your amplifier topology (tube or
 solid-state), and your listening preferences. A bright-sounding system
 paired with the wrong conductor choice can turn fatiguing; that
 conversation alone is worth the visit.
</p>
<h2>
 Which Cable Types Match Your System?
</h2>
<p>
 Before spending anything, sort out what you actually need. Most systems
 use a mix of:
</p>
<ul>
 <li>
 <strong>Speaker cables</strong>
 running from amp to speaker, usually 10 or 12 gauge for runs over
 eight feet.
 </li>
 <li>
 <strong>RCA interconnects</strong>
 between source components and a preamp, where shielding and
 capacitance matter a lot.
 </li>
 <li>
 <strong>XLR (balanced) interconnects</strong>
 for longer runs and gear with true balanced circuits, with better
 noise rejection than RCA.
 </li>
 <li>
 <strong>Power cords</strong>
 feeding the amp and source, where the debate gets loudest.
 </li>
 <li>
 <strong>Digital cables</strong>
 (coax, AES/EBU, USB) for streamers and DACs, where timing precision
 matters.
 </li>
</ul>
<h3>
 Speaker Cables
</h3>
<p>
 For most homes, a quality 12-gauge run is the sweet spot, stepping up
 to 10-gauge for long pulls or demanding loudspeakers. Cardas and
 Transparent both make excellent speaker cable with clear upgrade paths,
 and our own bench-built runs cover the cases the catalogues don't —
 odd lengths, bi-wire configurations, unusual terminations. There's more
 detail on our
 <a href="/products/cables-power/interconnects-speaker-cables">interconnects and speaker cables</a>
 page.
</p>
<h3>
 RCA and XLR Interconnects
</h3>
<p>
 If your electronics offer balanced connections, XLR is worth using for
 longer runs — its noise rejection is a genuine, measurable advantage.
 For RCA, shielding and capacitance matter most. We stock a deep range
 of Cardas interconnects and Transparent's The Link, and we build custom
 interconnects to order in either format, at any length, with your
 choice of connectors.
</p>
<h3>
 Power Cords and Conditioners
</h3>
<p>
 Power is where skeptics roll their eyes and enthusiasts swear they hear
 a quieter background. We sit somewhere in the middle. A well-shielded
 <a href="/products/cables-power/ac-power-cords">power cord</a>
 — the Shunyata Venom we keep in stock is a sensible first step — can
 audibly lower the noise floor in a system fed from a busy electrical
 panel, which describes a lot of older Glebe and Westboro homes. For
 deeper problems,
 <a href="/products/cables-power/power-line-conditioners">power conditioning and regeneration</a>
 go further: PS Audio's Power Plant regenerators rebuild the AC waveform
 outright rather than merely filtering it, taking the quality of your
 electrical service out of the equation entirely.
</p>
<h2>
 The Objectivist–Subjectivist Debate, and a Pragmatist's Answer
</h2>
<h3>
 The Measurement-First Camp
</h3>
<p>
 Objectivists argue that once a cable meets baseline electrical specs —
 adequate gauge, sound termination, proper shielding — further spending
 produces no measurable difference. They're right about something
 important: those fundamentals are measurable, they matter most, and no
 exotic material rescues a cable that gets them wrong.
</p>
<h3>
 The Listening-First Camp
</h3>
<p>
 Subjectivists counter that measurements don't capture everything our
 ears process over hours of listening. Owners of revealing
 electrostatics like the MartinLogans we carry often report distinct
 shifts in imaging and treble texture when they change reference
 cables. Anecdotal? Sure. Universal? Not at all. Real to the listener?
 Apparently yes.
</p>
<h3>
 Our Pragmatist Take
</h3>
<p>
 Coming from an analytical background, we hold both ideas at once. Get
 the measurable fundamentals right first, because they're not
 negotiable — then let your ears, in your own system, settle the rest.
 Diminishing returns arrive faster than the industry likes to admit: a
 $300 interconnect on a $1,500 system is probably misallocated capital,
 while the same cable on a $15,000 reference rig might be the missing
 link. Match cable spend to system resolution, and never pay for a
 difference you couldn't hear.
</p>
<h2>
 How to Choose the Right Cable for Your Budget
</h2>
<p>
 Roughly 5 to 10 percent of total system cost is a reasonable starting
 heuristic for cabling. Less than that and you may be bottlenecking
 components you paid good money for; much more and you're probably
 chasing ghosts. On a modest system, a set of bench-built custom
 interconnects and a properly gauged speaker run from our workshop
 covers the fundamentals for sensible money. As systems grow, Cardas and
 Transparent offer clear upgrade ladders, and clean-power investments
 like a PS Audio regenerator start earning their keep.
</p>
<p>
 Two more things worth knowing. Everything we sell is authorized-dealer
 stock, so manufacturer warranties are real. And our trade-in program
 applies to cables just as it does to components — as your system
 evolves, your cabling can evolve with it without starting from zero.
</p>
<h2>
 Why Book a Listening Demo Before You Buy
</h2>
<p>
 A demo does two things a spec sheet can't. First, it confirms whether
 you actually hear a difference, which is the only metric that matters
 when you're the one paying. Second, it lets you ask questions the
 internet won't answer honestly — because the internet has opinions,
 but it doesn't have your room, your gear, or your ears.
</p>
<p>
 Bring music you know intimately, not audiophile demo discs. A familiar
 vocal or a complex jazz piano recording will tell you more in three
 minutes than an hour of unfamiliar material. Cables won't fix a bad
 system, but they will either preserve or quietly degrade what a good
 one can do — and the only honest way to find out which cables earn a
 place in yours is to listen.
 <a href="/contact">Book a listening demo</a>
 at our Carling Avenue showroom and we'll set up comparisons on gear
 like yours, with your music, at whatever pace the decision needs.
</p>

## BLOCK 5946 (faqItem)

### question field-uid: 89415568-1e17-45b6-9a94-45633d1a9541
Do expensive cables really sound better?

### answer field-uid: 6882284a-6822-4a88-89bf-6f1a1b0f45fd
Sometimes — on a resolving system, to attentive ears, and rarely as dramatically as the marketing suggests. The measurable fundamentals (correct gauge, solid termination, proper shielding) account for most of a cable's performance, and they don't have to cost a fortune. Beyond that, differences between well-made cables like Cardas or Transparent are real to many listeners but subtle, and they depend heavily on your system and room. That's why we'd rather you audition than take anyone's word for it — ours included.

## BLOCK 5947 (faqItem)

### question field-uid: 89415568-1e17-45b6-9a94-45633d1a9541
What gauge speaker cable do I need?

### answer field-uid: 6882284a-6822-4a88-89bf-6f1a1b0f45fd
12 AWG covers runs under 20 feet in most home systems; step up to 10 AWG for longer pulls or low-impedance loudspeakers. Thinner cable adds resistance that can soften bass control on long runs, while going heavier than you need buys nothing audible. Because we build speaker cables to length in our Ottawa workshop, you can get exactly the gauge and run your room requires without paying for coiled excess.

## BLOCK 5949 (faqItem)

### question field-uid: 89415568-1e17-45b6-9a94-45633d1a9541
Should I bother with a power conditioner?

### answer field-uid: 6882284a-6822-4a88-89bf-6f1a1b0f45fd
If your system reveals background hash on quiet passages, yes — clean power is worth addressing. Older Ottawa homes in particular often have noisy electrical panels shared with appliances and dimmers. Options range from a well-shielded power cord like the Shunyata Venom to full regeneration with a PS Audio Power Plant, which rebuilds the AC waveform rather than just filtering it. If your system is already dead quiet, a conditioner is optional insurance rather than a necessity.
